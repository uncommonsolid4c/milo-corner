PART 2 — HANDS-ONThe SPRI lab: one slider, one size cutoff

SPRI cleanup is the step that happens between almost every other step, and its whole behaviour is set by one number: the ratio of bead buffer to sample. Drag it and watch which fragments survive. This is the “ratio matters” step, made visible.

0.8× — e.g. 80 µL beads into 100 µL sample

More buffer → more PEG crowding the solution → DNA precipitates onto the beads at smaller sizes. Less buffer → only the big fragments come down, and the small ones wash away.

Effective cutoff: ~210 bp — fragments at or above it are kept; below it, they're lost in the wash.

That's how adapter dimers (~120 bp of pure junk — adapter ligated to adapter, no insert) get removed: pick a ratio whose cutoff sits above the dimer and below your library. It's also why cleanup can't be skipped between steps: leftover ligase or free adapters would poison the next reaction.

Kept on the beadsWashed away

Bar height ∝ fragment length. The 120 bp bar is the adapter dimer — the thing you're usually trying to throw away without throwing away your library.

PART 3 — HANDS-ONThe PCR lab: amplification is not free

PCR is how a library gets enough copies to sequence — and it's the step with the honest caveat for oncology. Drag the cycle count and watch two costs grow with it: duplicate reads and GC bias.

10 cycles

Every cycle, primers matching the adapter sequences copy each ligated fragment — which is why only successfully ligated fragments amplify. Unligated DNA has no primer sites and stays invisible. The same primers also carry the sample indices, so this is where multiplexing is added: many samples, one run, told apart by barcode.

More cycles = more material from less input. But copies of copies aren't new information — and fragments don't copy equally.

GC-balanced fragments amplify happily; GC-rich ones (stuck-together, hard to melt apart) lag further behind every cycle. In oncology this is not academic: a variant in a GC-rich exon can be systematically under-counted, and duplicates inflate apparent depth without adding evidence — which is why variant calling marks duplicates and thinks about bias instead of trusting raw read counts.
